A letter from the Executive Team

Welcome aboard

Welcome to the first issue of the Nebraska Space, Cyber, and National Security Law Society newsletter.

The fields we study move quickly. A satellite operator's licensing question, a ransomware incident at a hospital system, a new export control rule — any one of these can reshape a practice area before a casebook catches up. Our goal with this newsletter is simple: give students at Nebraska Law a reliable, once-a-month place to see what is happening in space, cyber, and national security law, and what it means for the people who want to work in it.

This is a student publication, and it is built around what students actually need. Every issue will carry job, internship, and externship postings, along with fellowships, writing competitions, and deadlines worth putting on a calendar. Nebraska sits in an unusual position in this field, and the opportunities that come across our desks should not stop at the desks of whoever happens to hear about them first.

We also want this to be a place to publish. If you have written a case note, a seminar paper you are proud of, a reaction to a speaker, or an opinion on a policy question, send it to us. Space, cyber, and national security law is a small enough world that a well-argued student piece gets read by people who matter in it, and we would rather those eyes land on your work than not.

If there is something you want to see covered, an opportunity worth sharing, or a piece you want to submit, reach out. This newsletter will be as useful as the community makes it.

Sincerely,
The SCNS Law Society Executive Team

04 · Get published

Call for submissions

Are you a student who is interested in having your writing published? If so, we are looking for submissions!

Whether it’s commentary on technology policy, reactions to novel space activities, or notes on case law, we want to read what you are writing so we can share it with the rest of our community.

We will publish student submissions in the December 2026 Newsletter, so if you want your work to be included, please email it to Emily Bowling (ebowling2@huskers.unl.edu) by Monday, November 23, 2026. Since we must review everything that is being submitted, we ask that you keep the word count at or below 8,000 words and submit your work in document form (not PDF). This is a brand new thing for us, so we are open to lots of different submissions, so long as they are centered around law and policy and they meaningfully engage with space, cybersecurity, or national security. Importantly, you do not have to be a Nebraska Law student to contribute. If you have any questions about what sorts of topics are appropriate, or any questions about submissions at all, reach out to Emily Bowling.

05 · Mission log

D.C. trip highlights

This year’s theme: “Cislunar, the Moon, and Beyond”

In September, members of the SCNS Law Society traveled to Washington D.C. to attend the Nebraska Space Law Conference. Bailey Yoder (’28), Alyssa Hurta (’28), Benjamin Harnden (’28), Lester Younglove (’28), Emily Bowling (’27), Eilif Vanderkolk (LLM), and Erica LaVoy (LLM) joined Professors Erik Mudrinich, Elsbeth Magilton, and Frans von der Dunk to hear panelists talk about novel issues affecting the space community.

The theme of this year was “Cislunar, the Moon, and Beyond” and we heard Professor von der Dunk speak on lunar safety zones, Professor Steve Mirmina consider the explosion of commercial lunar activity, and Matias Cava led a panel in a discussion of regulatory hot topics like intellectual property issues, spectrum regulation, and the Office of Space Commerce’s new Space Commerce Certification program. The panel was comprised of Krystyna Colantoni, Leana Brown, and Kiersten Fortson.

Our Nebraska Law students also took two site visits while in D.C.; one to the Office of Space Commerce to talk with Matias Cava and his office about the Space Commerce Certification program, and the second to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration to learn from their Office of General Counsel about the work that NOAA does in space.
